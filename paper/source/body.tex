\begin{abstract}
Premise selection normally asks which lemmas may help one proof state.  We study
the shared-budget problem: which small lemma set best serves several related
goals at once?  A \method{} episode displays the types of 16 name-masked
candidates and eight name-masked targets, and asks for three candidates.  A
target is covered when at least one chosen candidate occurs directly in its
historical elaborated value.  We construct 30 development and 60 test episodes
from \mathlib{} v4.33.0, enumerate all 560 portfolios, retain every maximizer,
and independently replay the dependency labels in pinned Lean.  The public test
set and label digest were committed before the response captures were supplied.
\ResultAbstractSentence On common 20-episode ID sets, structured versus direct
scores were 1/20 versus 4/20 (Pro) and 0/20 versus 1/20 (xhigh); this $q=2$
pipeline did not improve exact accuracy.  The task measures compression of historical dependencies,
not proof success under added premises.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: one budget, several goals}

Suppose that a prover can place only three lemmas in a shared context for eight
related goals.  Choosing a plausible lemma for each goal independently can
waste that budget: a less obvious lemma may be useful across several goals.
The same choice arises when building a retrieval cache for a file or asking a
mathematician to inspect a short list of reusable lemmas.  We call this shared
selection problem the \emph{lemma-portfolio} problem.

Most premise retrievers are queried for one proof state.  LeanDojo and ReProver
make this setting explicit for Lean \citep{yang2023leandojo}, Magnushammer
studies the analogous problem for Isabelle \citep{mikula2024magnushammer}, and
recent Lean work also uses the dependency graph
\citep{petrovic2025structural}; LeanHammer adapts neural selection to local
contexts and couples it to proof reconstruction \citep{zhu2026leanhammer}.
UseFor asks the stronger utility question of
how a theorem changes proof likelihood \citep{kasriel2025usefor}.  LeanSearch
v2 retrieves a premise group for one whole theorem \citep{gao2026leansearchv2},
while miniCTX studies theorem proving with long project context
\citep{hu2025minictx}.  TheoremBench expands one theorem into related proving
tasks and reports partial progress \citep{pham2026theorembench}, but does not
impose one shared premise budget across them.  We instead isolate the finite
decision that comes before several proofs: one fixed-size portfolio from a
closed pool must serve all of them.  To our knowledge, the formal-premise
benchmarks reviewed above do not score this multi-target, shared-budget
objective with complete exact optima.

The system must infer a hidden pattern of historical reuse from formal
statements alone.  Because the pool is small, we can enumerate every portfolio
and credit every maximizer rather than privilege one answer.  Our contribution
is threefold: a precise shared-budget objective, a module-disjoint and exactly
audited \mathlib{} benchmark, and an evaluation that separates exact portfolio
choice from partial coverage and support prediction.  The benchmark is not a
new prover or a claim about logical necessity.

\section{Task, scoring, and construction}

An episode contains a candidate pool $C$ with $|C|=16$, a target set $T$ with
$|T|=8$, and a budget $B=3$.  Candidate and target declaration names are
replaced by identifiers \code{C01}--\code{C16} and
\code{T01}--\code{T08}; candidate types and target statements remain visible.
We use ``lemma'' informally for a candidate declaration, which may also be a
definition or constructor.
For a target $t$, let
\[
 D(t)=\{c\in C : c\text{ occurs as a direct constant in the elaborated value
 of }t\}.
\]
Occurrence is syntactic in the elaborated declaration body; we take no
transitive closure.  For a theorem, this body is its elaborated proof term.
For a portfolio $S\subseteq C$ of size $B$, its historical target coverage is
\begin{equation}
  F(S)=\bigl|\{t\in T:D(t)\cap S\ne\varnothing\}\bigr|.       \label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
Let $F^*=\max_{|S|=B}F(S)$ and
$\mathcal A=\{S:|S|=B,\,F(S)=F^*\}$.  We enumerate all
$\binom{16}{3}=560$ portfolios and reconstruct $\mathcal A$.  The primary test
score is the number $n$ of the 60 episodes for which the submitted portfolio is
in $\mathcal A$, reported as $n/60$ and a percentage.  Thus every maximizing
portfolio is correct.  The secondary item scores are raw target coverage
$F(S)$ (also $F(S)/8$), normalized coverage $F(S)/F^*$, and direct-edge recall
\[
 R_E(S)=\frac{\sum_{t\in T}|D(t)\cap S|}{\sum_{t\in T}|D(t)|}.
\]
Aggregate edge recall is micro-averaged by summing this numerator and
denominator over episodes.
A missing, malformed, wrong-budget, duplicate-ID, or out-of-pool response gets
zero on all four scores and remains in the 60-item denominator; validity is
reported separately.

This is a small maximum-coverage problem \citep{nemhauser1978submodular}, but
the incidence graph is hidden behind the displayed statements.  If \code{C01}
serves three targets, \code{C02} two others, and \code{C03} a sixth, their
portfolio covers six; spending two slots on alternatives for the first three
targets can waste the budget.  The $16/8/3$ design keeps prompts readable and
all choices enumerable while still rewarding reuse.

\paragraph{Source and construction.}
We mine target values and candidate types from \mathlib{} v4.33.0 at commit
\code{db584cd6d46c92f209a44c0f1c829460d327499d}
\citep{mathlib2020,mathlib433commit}.  Each episode comes from one module, with
names hidden.  Development, test, and all earlier prototype modules and
statements are disjoint.  We reject simple ``largest star'' instances: each
retained episode has at least seven active candidates, at least four active
candidates outside every optimum, multiple multi-edge targets, and multiple
near-optimal portfolios; every member of an optimum is reused and essential to
that portfolio.  The complete thresholds are in the supplement.

The miner also rejects any episode solved by any of 20 fixed construction
methods.  Eighteen use only public strings or positions; two use the true
incidence graph and rank candidates by frequency or greedy maximum coverage.
These methods define the challenge distribution and are not held-out baselines.
Among the 60 test episodes, 45 have $F^*=6$ and 15 have $F^*=7$; together they
have 78 accepted optimal portfolios.  The final split contains 30 development
and 60 test episodes across \BlindDomainCount{} top-level domains.

\paragraph{Commitment and label audit.}
The underlying theorem statements are public and may occur in model training,
so we do not claim contamination-free evaluation.  What is new is their
composition into name-masked episodes and the hidden incidence rows.  On 1
September 2026 we recorded the public-file hash and a digest of the label file;
the response captures used below were supplied afterward.  The prompts do not
contain the labels.  A separate pinned-Lean 4.33.0 replay reconstructed every
displayed edge, and scoring freshly reconstructs every optimum.  The response
PDFs lack provider logs and per-chat timestamps, so the deployment rows are
external captures with a prior dataset commitment, not a cryptographic blind
evaluation.  The supplement states this evidence boundary precisely.

\section{Experimental setup}

We ask three questions.  How often does a displayed deployment select an exact
optimum?  How much of the target set does it cover when it misses?  Finally,
does making the hidden target--candidate relation explicit improve the joint
decision?  The two author-supplied PDFs report six visible consumer labels:
GPT SOL 5.6 Pro and xhigh, two DeepSeek DeepThink modes, and Qwen 3.8 Max and
3.7 Plus in Thinking mode.  Each direct row is intended to cover all 60
episodes in twelve five-item response blocks.  We align responses by explicit episode
IDs, preserve malformed or missing blocks as zeroes, and make no repair.  The
analysis retains at most one supplied capture per intended block; anomalies
remain zero-scored, and no controlled repeated sampling is available.

The sole model-facing development check used the first 15 development episodes:
a controlled Codex CLI Sol capture obtained 6/15 exact optima.  It motivated no
change to the committed test set and is not pooled with the consumer rows.

Consumer interfaces do not expose the serving checkpoint, system prompt,
decoding policy, or routing decisions.  We therefore report the visible labels
with capitalization and punctuation normalized.  The convenience sample is neither representative nor a ranking of
providers or model families.

Two non-model references were fixed in advance.  Under uniform selection,
every portfolio has probability $1/560$, so the probability of an optimum is
$|\mathcal A|/560$.  If $d_t=|D(t)|$, the expected episode coverage is
\[
 \mathbb E[F(S)]=\sum_{t\in T}\left(1-
 \frac{\binom{16-d_t}{3}}{\binom{16}{3}}\right),
\]
and expected edge recall is $3/16$; no Monte Carlo estimate is needed.  A
character 3--5-gram TF--IDF reference instead enumerates all portfolios and
maximizes summed target-to-candidate facility-location similarity.  It was run
only after the split commitment and never used to admit an item.  The
supplement gives the full calculations.

\section{Diagnosing errors with structured support prediction}

A direct portfolio does not show whether Sol inferred the target--candidate
relations incorrectly or combined plausible relations poorly.  For each GPT
row, four additional responses cover the same 20 test episodes and return up
to four candidate IDs per target, ordered by plausibility.  A deterministic
program keeps the first two per target ($q=2$), then exactly solves the induced
size-three coverage problem; later ranks diagnose the inferred incidence graph.

\InterventionResultParagraph

These are error-localization diagnostics, not additional model rows.  They
compare direct prompting with the complete structured-prompt-plus-solver
pipeline.  We report item and whole-block differences without a significance
test.  Since the PDFs do not certify paired-call chronology, the contrasts are
descriptive rather than causal.

\section{Limitations and ways forward}

\method{} compresses one historical direct-dependency graph.  Direct occurrence
is reproducible, but it is neither logical necessity nor evidence that supplying
a lemma will cause a new proof to succeed.  The pool is closed, all costs are
uniform, and the 60 modules form a deliberately conditioned stress set.  Public
statements may have appeared in training.  Consumer routing is opaque, the
response PDFs supply no per-call operational evidence or controlled repetitions,
and we have no human-expert baseline.  Intervals therefore describe this fixed set;
they do not estimate performance over \mathlib{} or generation variance.

The natural next step is to train and evaluate set-level retrievers rather than
assemble per-goal rankings after the fact.  Predicted incidence can still be
useful, but this $q=2$ result leaves the respective contributions of calibration,
truncation, and tie-breaking unresolved.  Larger candidate pools, nonuniform context costs,
causal proof-success labels, repeated runs, and a mathematician baseline would
move the benchmark closer to prover practice.

\section{Conclusion}

Choosing one shared budget across several goals is a finite decision distinct
from per-goal retrieval, and it admits exact scoring with every tie preserved.

data, released labels, response transcription, scorer, Lean replay receipts,
